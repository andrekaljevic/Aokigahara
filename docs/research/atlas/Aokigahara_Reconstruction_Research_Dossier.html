How to use this dossier

Do not treat “Aokigahara” as one perfectly defined polygon. Sources quote roughly 30–35 km² because the name can refer to a modern forested landscape, the Jōgan/Aokigahara lava field, a protected/natural-monument context, or a tourism-defined area. For a faithful digital reconstruction, align several layers instead of forcing one outline.
1. Geology first

Use GSJ vectors/raster to place the AD 864–866 Aokigahara/Jōgan lava units, older cones and flow boundaries.

2. Terrain second

Use GSI LiDAR/elevation/shaded-relief material to recover lava lobes, flow fronts, fissure rows and micro-relief hidden by canopy.

3. Modern surface

Use current GSI aerial imagery plus OSM/local maps for canopy edge, roads, tracks, car parks, paths, buildings and current visitor infrastructure.

4. Ground truth

Use the image atlas below to match tree density, trunk scale, roots over basalt, moss/lichen, light penetration, path surfacing, cave entrances and lake-edge views.

5. Validate by viewpoints

Reproduce Sankodai/Koyodai/Lake Sai macro views and check skyline, relative lake positions, Mt Omuro and Mount Fuji alignment.

Reconstruction priorities the image corpus should answer

Macro silhouette

Forest–lake boundaries, visible cones/ridges, Mt Fuji direction, Mt Omuro scale and relationship to the Five Lakes.

Canopy structure

Mixed conifer/broadleaf composition, trunk spacing, vertical density, irregular crown profile and seasonal variation.

Ground morphology

Shallow soil, exposed basalt, collapsed/ropy lava, hummocks, surface roots, moss carpets and low understorey.

Light & atmosphere

Canopy occlusion, muted indirect light, dappled shafts, humidity/green bounce, winter snow contrast and autumn colour.

Human layer

Route 139, prefectural/forest roads, narrow surfaced tracks, signed paths, cave visitor structures, fences and car parks.

Subsurface

Three principal tourist lava caves plus other mapped protected caves; treat exact accessible/non-accessible status from the 2026 guideline map as authoritative.

Reconstruction blueprint — geography, sectors & confidence

Do not use one polygon for “Aokigahara”. The official tourism forest is about 3,000 ha / 30 km², while the nationally designated Natural Monument “Fuji Primeval Forest and Aokigahara Jukai” is about 1,600 ha after an approximately 816 ha addition in 2010. The 864 lava field, national-park protection zones and present canopy are related but not identical. The final build should preserve these as separate GIS layers.

Forest footprint

Present woodland / canopy used for the visual world. Yamanashi describes Aokigahara as roughly 3,000 ha.

Protected cultural-natural area

About 1,600 ha designated as the national Natural Monument “Fuji Primeval Forest and Aokigahara Jukai”; not the full forest.

864 lava field

Model from GSJ geology + GSI volcanic-landform data. Do not infer it from canopy colour alone.

Playable/context AOI

Use a broader ingest window around the Three Lakes, Route 139 and Fuji slope, then clip by the authoritative layers. A useful ingest window is roughly 138.55–138.72°E, 35.43–35.53°N; this is deliberately not claimed as a forest boundary.

Coordinate and terrain pipeline

For production, work in a metre-based Japan Plane Rectangular Zone VIII grid (the Yamanashi zone), retaining WGS84/JGD geographic coordinates for interchange. GSI's 2026 download service warns that current data are transitioning from JGD2011 to JGD2024, so the ingest process must read source metadata and transform everything to one chosen project datum rather than silently mixing them.

GSI currently provides 1 m, 5 m and 10 m DEM products. The 1 m and 5 m products can be derived from airborne laser survey; GSI states that its DEM is bare earth with buildings, bridges and trees removed. For Aokigahara, first test 1 m coverage; otherwise use 5 m DEM5A wherever available, then 5 m photogrammetric DEM, with 10 m only as a fallback. Overlay GSI's Fuji volcanic-landform classification and the GSJ geological map before any procedural smoothing.

Geology that must survive the terrain build

The A.D. 864 Aokigahara Lava Flow is described in volcanological research as two major flows: Ishizuka Lava, from a vent at the foot of Mt Omuro, and Nagaoyama Lava, from the Nagaoyama cinder cone. At the Motosu front, research describes about 36 protruding flow lobes that pinch, swell and sometimes bifurcate, with clefts, clinker surfaces and distinct flow-lobe morphologies. A photoreal recreation should therefore retain lobe fronts, fissures, shallow depressions and lava shelves rather than reduce the floor to generic noise.

Vegetation should be spatially heterogeneous

A Tokyo University survey of 43 plots distinguished seven forest types: open Pinus, Pinus, Tsuga–Pinus, Tsuga, Chamaecyparis–Tsuga, Chamaecyparis and deciduous broadleaved forest. Tsuga forest occupied a large part of the study area; Chamaecyparis-dominant communities occurred toward higher elevations, while deciduous broadleaved forest was concentrated on deeper-soil sites that escaped lava cover. This is strong evidence against using one uniform “Aokigahara biome”.

A separate 0.25 ha permanent plot found Chamaecyparis obtusa at 45.4% of basal area and Tsuga sieboldii at 26.3%, with stem densities of 968 and 292 ha⁻¹ respectively. Those figures are useful local calibration targets, but should not be extrapolated across the whole forest.

Sector plan

S1 · Motosu south-west lava front

Ishizuka lava lobes, Motosu lakeshore and Motosu wind-cave group. Highest-value terrain/geology validation sector.

S2 · Shōji / north-west transition

Lake Shōji edge, Shōji entrance trail and forest/lake transitions.

S3 · Mt Omuro base

Fuji, Shōjiko, Omuro, Jinza, Kamaboko and Megane caves, former transport roads and the beech-square area shown on Yamanashi's official schematic.

S4 · Upper south-east flow / Karuimizu

Higher-elevation forest, Karuimizu cave morphology and forest-road network.

S5 · Route 139 cave corridor

Fugaku Wind Cave, Narusawa Ice Cave, roadside edge, car parks and visitor infrastructure. Best visual coverage.

S6 · Lake Sai south / bat-cave corridor

Lake Saiko Bat Cave, Ryugu Cave, Wild Bird Forest and the tourist-home-village approach.

S7 · Nenba / Saiko edge

Lava-to-lake transitions and the human landscape at the north edge.

S8 · Sankodai/Koyodai panorama belt

Macro canopy silhouette, three-lake context and Mount Fuji alignment. Use for camera-match QA.

S9 · Shōji entrance ascent

Southern route continuity toward Fuji and higher-elevation biome transition. This remains one of the largest evidence gaps.

S10 · Outer context shell

Lakes, roads, villages and surrounding ridges needed so free roam never reaches a fake horizon.

What still blocks a claim of “perfect recreation”

The biggest remaining uncertainty is route-continuous, metre-scale ground truth away from the main public corridors. Exact tree placement, deadwood, understorey, micro-paths and sub-metre lava relief are not globally documented. The build can be geographically correct at terrain/landform/route scale and statistically/ecologically faithful at vegetation scale, but it should only reproduce individual trees/rocks exactly where geolocated evidence supports that claim.

Next research priority: acquire/check the actual GSI DEM mesh coverage for the broad ingest AOI, align the official 2026 route/cave schematic to the metric project grid, and build a coverage heat-map of geolocated images/video/GPX by 250 m cell. That will expose the remaining blind spots immediately.

Current route evidence

Yamanashi's 2026 schematic explicitly distinguishes usable routes, former transport roads/forest roads and protected lava caves around Mt Omuro and Lake Sai. It is a primary official topology source, but it is schematic rather than survey-grade. The correct workflow is to georegister it to stable controls, report the residual error, and replace individual segments with GSI or public GPX geometry where available.

Fugaku ↔ Narusawa

A current YAMAP model course reports 2.5 km, 1:02 and 58 m of ascent/descent, substantially strengthening route-continuity evidence along the best-documented public corridor.

Cave corridor → Sankodai

A 2026 YAMAP activity records 11.8 km and orders Fugaku Wind Cave → Narusawa Ice Cave → Koyodai → Sankodai before continuing to Ashiwadayama.

Shōji entrance

Current YAMAP activity listings include a 10.8 km Shōji entrance trail hike dated 10 August 2026 plus longer routes through Aokigahara/Omuro. This is now a priority discovery source for the western/central continuity gap.

What this changes

The project can now distinguish three different questions cell-by-cell: do we have terrain/vegetation source coverage?, do we have route-continuous evidence?, and do we have visual ground truth close enough to validate a build? That is much closer to a digital-twin research workflow than counting photographs.

Next hard step: download and catalogue the actual GSI elevation mesh files and Ministry vegetation Shapefiles, then replace these evidence heuristics with direct per-cell data coverage and the real vegetation polygons. Until that happens, the 250 m map is a research-priority instrument, not a claim of metre-perfect reconstruction.

Modern vegetation rules for the build

  • Use the 2024 dataset's legend name/code to assign biome families and species/community mixes.
  • Retain each polygon's 作成年度 / creation year; do not present old polygon observations as if surveyed in 2024.
  • Use current orthophotography to detect canopy clearings, road-edge changes and large disturbances after the polygon source year.
  • Never use a vegetation polygon to assert the precise position, diameter or species of an individual tree.

Route reconstruction rules

Yamanashi's pilgrimage research states that the Shōji-guchi route starts at Akaike on the south side of Lake Shōji, passes through Aokigahara and around Mt Omuro/Nagao before reaching Komitake; the complete route is over 17 km and climbs almost 1,400 m. It also explicitly describes the Narusawa-michi crossing the Shōji-guchi route. These relationships are now stored separately from geometry confidence.

The official eco-tour schematic further distinguishes the Shōji-guchi trail, former haul roads, forest roads, public walking paths and protected lava caves around Mt Omuro and Lake Sai. This is the right source for connectivity, but every centreline still needs GSI/vector/GPX replacement before a final build is frozen.

Terrain verification status

The previous DEM tile plan remains a candidate acquisition plan. GSI confirms DEM1A is the most accurate published class (~1 m, airborne laser) and that the map/elevation service falls back through DEM5A/B/C to DEM10B where higher-resolution data are absent. A constructed DEM1A URL is therefore not evidence that a 1 m tile exists at that location. Bulk tile or GML ingestion still has to record actual no-data/availability cell by cell.

Current strongest reconstruction sectors

The public Route 139 cave corridor (Fugaku–Narusawa), the Lake Sai/Bat Cave/Ryugu sector and the Shōji-guchi corridor now have mutually reinforcing official route, recent visual and environmental evidence. The least defensible areas for exact object placement remain the deep central/western cells away from mapped public routes and geolocated imagery.

Expansion 5 — temporal aerial atlas, geology stack & boundary separation

This pass attacks a different failure mode: silently treating one outline as “Aokigahara”. The project now has a dedicated boundary register that keeps the research AOI, present forest/canopy, Jōgan lava field, national Natural Monument, Fuji-Hakone-Izu National Park zoning, forest-management compartments and administrative boundaries separate.

Key legal boundary fact: Yamanashi records the National Natural Monument Fuji Primeval Forest and Aokigahara Jukai as originally designated in 1926, expanded by about 816 ha in 2010, with a designated extent of about 1,600 ha. That protected area is not synonymous with the whole forest.

Time-series aerial evidence

GSI exposes current seamless aerial photography, 2007+ orthophotos/annual photography, and historical aerial mosaics for 1987–90, 1984–86, 1979–83, 1974–78 and 1961–69. These are now catalogued as a temporal evidence stack. Their existence does not guarantee that every cell is populated in every era, so local coverage is still a QA step.

Volcanic terrain and geology

The live atlas now exposes GSI volcanic basic-map, coloured-relief, photo-map, volcanic land-condition and Fuji-specific digital geomorphology layers. It also now includes the official GSJ/AIST 1:50,000 Fuji Volcano Geological Map (2nd ed., 2016) WMTS, not merely the lower-resolution national seamless geology. GSJ publishes the underlying 2016 map as an 8.6 MB vector Shapefile as well; that remains the preferred future source for extracting the Jōgan/Aokigahara lava units as real vector geometry rather than drawing them by eye.

Why historical photography matters

For a present-day recreation, historical photographs are not used to place 1960s trees into a 2026 scene. They expose which straight edges, roads, clearings and canopy discontinuities are persistent, which are recent, and where vegetation or visitor infrastructure has changed. This reduces the risk of “baking in” a transient feature from one modern image.
